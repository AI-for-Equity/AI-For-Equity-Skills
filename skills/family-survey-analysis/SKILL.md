# Family Survey Analysis

Analyze family and caregiver survey data and produce a concise, evidence-based leadership memo.

## Inputs

Accept any combination of:

- CSV or XLSX survey exports
- Likert-scale or other quantitative survey results
- Open-ended responses
- Pasted survey data or summaries
- Survey questions or survey instruments
- School, grade, program, language, demographic, or other subgroup fields, when available
- Prior-year or comparison data, when available

Ask for the survey data if it has not yet been provided.

When useful, also ask for:

- the purpose of the survey
- the population invited to participate
- number of respondents
- response rate, if known
- whether responses represent households, individual caregivers, or students
- whether families may have responded more than once for multiple children
- relevant school or district context
- prior survey results or benchmarks

Do not require information that is unnecessary to begin the analysis.

## Analysis Principles

### Separate evidence from interpretation

Clearly distinguish:

1. what the data directly shows
2. reasonable interpretations of that evidence
3. hypotheses that would require additional evidence

Do not present hypotheses as findings.

### Assess strength of evidence

For every major finding, indicate how strongly the available data supports it.

Use plain-language evidence-strength labels:

- **Strong evidence**
- **Moderate evidence**
- **Emerging signal**
- **Insufficient evidence**

Base the assessment on factors such as:

- sample size
- response rate
- representativeness of respondents
- consistency across related questions
- magnitude of the pattern
- consistency between quantitative and qualitative evidence
- number and diversity of comments supporting a theme
- subgroup sample sizes
- availability of comparison or trend data

Explain briefly why each judgment was assigned.

### Use statistical confidence intervals appropriately

When sufficient quantitative data is available, calculate and report an appropriate confidence interval for important estimates such as proportions or means.

Do not call a qualitative evidence-strength judgment a "confidence interval."

If a statistically defensible confidence interval cannot be calculated from the available data, say so rather than inventing one.

### Consider representativeness carefully

Family surveys are especially vulnerable to participation bias.

When possible, examine whether respondents appear representative of the broader family population by factors such as:

- school
- grade level
- home language
- program participation
- student demographic group
- other available characteristics

Do not assume that survey respondents represent all families.

Call out groups that appear underrepresented when the available data supports doing so.

Do not treat a low response rate as evidence that nonrespondents are satisfied or dissatisfied.

### Analyze quantitative data

When applicable:

- calculate response distributions
- summarize central tendencies where meaningful
- identify unusually high or low items
- identify meaningful differences across related items
- examine year-over-year changes when comparison data exists
- examine subgroup differences when appropriate
- distinguish meaningful patterns from minor numerical variation

Do not imply statistical significance unless it has actually been tested.

### Analyze open-ended responses

Identify recurring themes, tensions, strengths, concerns, and notable minority perspectives.

Look for:

- themes appearing across multiple respondents
- comments that help explain quantitative findings
- contradictions or divergent experiences
- issues that may be important despite appearing infrequently
- examples that illuminate a broader pattern

Do not treat word frequency as equivalent to importance.

Paraphrase comments by default.

Use direct quotations only when they meaningfully clarify a finding and cannot reasonably identify the respondent, student, or staff member.

### Handle subgroup analysis carefully

Analyze differences by school, grade, program, language, demographic group, or other categories only when sample sizes make the comparison responsible.

Do not surface results for very small groups when doing so could risk identifying families or students.

Avoid making claims about why groups differ unless the survey provides evidence for the explanation.

Pay particular attention to cases where the overall result appears positive but particular groups report meaningfully different experiences.

## Family Experience

Identify what the survey suggests about families' experience of the school or system.

Consider themes such as:

- trust
- communication
- responsiveness
- academic expectations
- quality of instruction
- student support
- safety
- belonging
- inclusion
- relationships with teachers and staff
- leadership
- access to information
- opportunities for family engagement
- cultural and linguistic responsiveness
- confidence in the school's direction
- other themes emerging from the data

Do not force the analysis into these categories when the survey evidence points elsewhere.

## Enrollment, Retention, and External Messaging

Analyze what the survey may suggest about enrollment, re-enrollment, family advocacy, and external communications.

Do not treat satisfaction data alone as proof that a family will enroll, re-enroll, leave, or recommend the school.

Distinguish among:

### Strong Family Experience Assets

Identify aspects of the family experience that are clearly supported by the survey and could credibly be highlighted in enrollment or community communications.

For each asset:

1. State the supporting evidence.
2. Assess the strength of that evidence.
3. Identify which families or groups appear to share the experience.
4. Note any contradictory evidence or caveats.

### Promising Themes

Identify positive signals that may have value for enrollment or communications but require additional validation before becoming major public claims.

### Experience Gaps and Retention Risks

Identify areas where:

- dissatisfaction is meaningful
- trust appears weak
- particular groups report substantially different experiences
- the desired school promise is not supported by current family experience
- unresolved concerns could plausibly affect family confidence or retention

Describe these as potential risks unless direct evidence about enrollment or re-enrollment intent is available.

### Enrollment & Communications Implications

Suggest ways enrollment, recruitment, or communications teams could accurately translate supported strengths into family-facing messaging.

Where useful, suggest candidate language.

Do not recommend claims that exaggerate or contradict the survey evidence.

Identify messages the organization should avoid or qualify.

## Recommended Actions

Translate the findings into practical recommendations.

Organize recommendations into:

### Quick / Easy Wins

Actions that could reasonably be implemented with limited time, coordination, or resources.

Examples might include:

- communication changes
- clarifying processes or expectations
- improving translation or language access
- making information easier to find
- closing feedback loops
- adjusting communication frequency or channels
- improving access to existing resources

### Near-Term Priorities

Actions requiring some planning, ownership, or cross-functional coordination.

### Longer-Term Priorities

Issues requiring structural change, budget decisions, policy changes, sustained leadership attention, or deeper investigation.

For each recommendation, connect the action explicitly to the survey evidence.

Do not recommend an action merely because it is a general best practice.

When evidence is insufficient to recommend an intervention, recommend a next question or additional investigation instead.

## Output

Produce a leadership memo using this structure:

# Family Survey Analysis

## Executive Summary

Provide a concise executive summary containing:

- the 3–5 most important findings
- the overall picture emerging from the survey
- the most consequential immediate actions
- the most important enrollment or retention implication, if supported
- any major limitation leaders should keep in mind

Keep this section short enough for a senior leader to understand the central message quickly.

## Survey Context

Summarize:

- number of respondents
- response rate, when known
- survey population
- whether responses represent households or individual caregivers, when known
- types of questions analyzed
- relevant comparison data
- major limitations
- known representativeness concerns

## Key Findings

For each major finding include:

**Finding:**  
State the finding clearly.

**Evidence:**  
Describe the quantitative and/or qualitative evidence.

**Strength of evidence:**  
Strong / Moderate / Emerging / Insufficient

**Why:**  
Briefly explain the confidence assessment.

**Statistical interval, when applicable:**  
Provide the confidence interval and methodology when a defensible interval can be calculated.

**Interpretation:**  
Explain what the evidence may mean, clearly distinguishing interpretation from observed fact.

## Themes from Open-Ended Responses

Summarize:

- recurring strengths
- recurring concerns
- important tensions or divergent experiences
- minority perspectives worth leadership attention
- qualitative context that helps explain quantitative findings

Avoid overstating how representative individual comments are.

## Family Experience

Describe the strongest signals related to:

- trust
- communication and responsiveness
- student experience
- belonging and inclusion
- academic experience
- relationships
- other important themes arising from the survey

Highlight meaningful differences among groups when responsible to do so.

## Enrollment, Retention & Communications Implications

### Strong Family Experience Assets

Identify experiences sufficiently supported to consider highlighting externally.

### Promising Themes

Identify positive signals requiring further validation.

### Experience Gaps & Retention Risks

Identify concerns or divergent experiences that could undermine trust or retention.

### Enrollment & Communications Implications

Suggest accurate ways supported strengths could inform enrollment, recruitment, or family communications.

Identify claims that should be avoided or qualified.

## Recommended Actions

### Quick / Easy Wins

Prioritize actions that can be implemented relatively quickly.

### Near-Term Priorities

Identify actions requiring additional planning or coordination.

### Longer-Term Priorities

Identify structural or strategic issues requiring sustained attention.

For every action, state which finding or evidence supports the recommendation.

## Questions for Further Investigation

Identify important questions the survey cannot answer and what additional evidence would help answer them.

Pay particular attention to:

- underrepresented families
- unexplained subgroup differences
- possible barriers to participation
- questions about enrollment or re-enrollment intent that the survey did not directly ask

## Methodological Notes and Limitations

Clearly state limitations involving:

- response rate
- representativeness
- sample size
- subgroup size
- missing data
- duplicate household responses
- survey design
- language accessibility
- digital access or participation barriers
- lack of benchmarks
- inability to establish causation
- other relevant constraints

## Final Quality Check

Before completing the memo:

- Verify that every major claim is supported by the supplied data.
- Separate observed evidence from interpretation.
- Do not invent statistics or respondents.
- Do not imply causation from correlation.
- Do not imply statistical significance without testing it.
- Do not expose personally identifiable information.
- Do not overgeneralize from small subgroups or isolated comments.
- Do not assume respondents represent all families.
- Make uncertainty visible rather than hiding it.
- Ensure recommendations follow from the evidence.
- Ensure enrollment and communications claims reflect actual family experience.
- Avoid deficit-based descriptions of families or communities.
- Do not interpret lower survey participation as lower family engagement without evidence.
- Write in clear, concise language suitable for school or district leadership.
