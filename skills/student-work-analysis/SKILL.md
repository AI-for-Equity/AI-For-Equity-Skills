# Student Work Analysis

Analyze a representative set of student work to help an educator understand what students know, where their thinking is breaking down, and what to do next instructionally.

Keep the analysis educator-facing. Do not generate student-facing feedback unless the user explicitly asks for a separate task.

## Workflow

1. Establish the task and criteria for success.
2. Inspect the representative set of student work.
3. Identify demonstrated understanding and recurring patterns.
4. Develop hypotheses about root misunderstandings behind errors.
5. Check whether the task, directions, language, or format may be contributing to the pattern.
6. Recommend quick checks that could confirm or disconfirm each hypothesis.
7. Recommend targeted instructional responses and flexible groupings.
8. Produce a concise educator-facing analysis.

## Inputs

Accept any combination of:

- essays or extended writing
- short constructed responses
- math work or problem-solving
- science explanations, models, or lab responses
- projects or performance tasks
- multiple-choice or selected-response work when reasoning or answer patterns are available
- rubrics, scoring guides, answer keys, or exemplars
- teacher annotations or scores
- scanned or photographed handwritten work
- pasted student responses
- a representative sample drawn from one or more classes or sections

Use only the student work and context actually supplied.

If handwriting, images, or scans are unclear, identify what cannot be read rather than guessing.

## Establish Criteria for Success

Before evaluating the work, determine what successful performance should look like.

Use available evidence in this order:

1. explicit criteria supplied by the educator
2. rubric or scoring guide
3. answer key
4. exemplar
5. task directions or prompt
6. a clear description of the intended learning goal or successful response

If the criteria for success can be reasonably inferred from the supplied materials, proceed without asking an unnecessary question.

If the criteria cannot be inferred, ask the user to provide criteria for success. Explain that these may take the form of a rubric, answer sheet, exemplar, learning goal, or brief description of what students should know or be able to do.

Do not invent hidden success criteria.

## Analyze the Representative Set

Treat the supplied work as a representative set only to the extent the user indicates that it is representative.

State the number of student responses analyzed when it can be determined.

When identifying a pattern, quantify it whenever possible using language such as:

- 7 of 12 responses
- about half of the sample
- 3 students showed this pattern
- this appeared in nearly every response reviewed

Do not convert the sample into a claim about an entire class, grade, school, or student population unless the sampling information supports that generalization.

## Identify What Students Understand

Start with evidence of successful thinking.

Identify:

- concepts students appear to understand
- strategies students use successfully
- parts of the criteria for success that are consistently met
- productive partial understandings
- strong or sophisticated approaches that may be useful as instructional examples

Support conclusions with concrete evidence from the work.

Do not reduce the analysis to a deficit list.

## Identify Recurring Errors and Root Understandings

Do not stop at naming an error.

For each important recurring error or incomplete response:

1. Describe the observable pattern.
2. Quantify how often it appears when possible.
3. Identify the underlying concept, assumption, strategy, or misunderstanding that could plausibly produce the pattern.
4. Label the root cause as a hypothesis unless the student work directly establishes it.
5. Identify evidence that supports the hypothesis.
6. Identify alternative explanations when they are plausible.
7. Suggest a quick check, question, or task that could confirm or disconfirm the hypothesis.

Prefer explanations of student thinking over labels.

For example, prefer:

> Several responses are consistent with students treating the denominator as the number selected rather than the total number of equal parts.

over:

> Students do not understand fractions.

Do not infer a broad deficiency from one error pattern.

## Distinguish Evidence From Interpretation

Separate:

- **Observed evidence:** what is visible in the student work
- **Interpretation:** what that pattern may suggest about student thinking
- **Hypothesis:** a possible root misunderstanding that requires further evidence

Use appropriately cautious language such as:

- appears to
- is consistent with
- may indicate
- suggests a possible misunderstanding of

Do not use uncertainty language when the evidence is direct and clear, but do not present interpretations as facts.

## Consider the Task as a Possible Source of Difficulty

Before attributing a pattern entirely to student understanding, examine whether the task itself may be contributing.

Consider:

- ambiguous directions
- unclear or hidden criteria for success
- unnecessary reading or vocabulary demands
- confusing formatting
- inaccessible representations
- unfamiliar terminology
- a prompt that requires skills beyond the intended learning target
- answer choices or examples that may cue a misconception
- insufficient information
- scoring criteria that reward something different from the stated learning goal

If the task appears to be contributing to errors, describe that as a separate finding rather than treating every error as evidence of a student misconception.

Do not assume language-production difficulty is the same as content misunderstanding when the evidence does not support that conclusion.

## Recommend Instructional Responses

For each important root-understanding hypothesis, recommend a targeted instructional response.

Connect each recommendation directly to the evidence.

Include, when useful:

- what concept or strategy to revisit
- a concrete representation or example to use
- a short reteach move
- guided practice
- a contrasting example or non-example
- a question that surfaces student reasoning
- an extension for students already demonstrating the target understanding
- a quick formative check after instruction

Do not recommend generic strategies merely because they are common instructional practices.

Explain why the proposed move addresses the hypothesized misunderstanding.

## Recommend Flexible Instructional Groupings

When the patterns support it, suggest temporary instructional groupings based on demonstrated needs or strategies.

Group students by the specific evidence in their work, not by fixed labels or perceived ability.

Use descriptions such as:

- students who are applying the correct procedure but cannot yet explain why it works
- students whose responses suggest confusion between area and perimeter
- students already meeting the success criteria and ready for an extension

Avoid labels such as:

- low students
- weak students
- struggling kids
- high kids
- advanced kids

Treat groupings as flexible and task-specific.

If the provided student work includes names or identifiers, minimize repeating them. Prefer anonymous group descriptions unless the educator specifically needs student-level grouping information.

## Guardrails

Never infer from student work alone:

- intelligence
- fixed ability
- motivation
- effort
- laziness
- disability
- diagnosis
- home environment
- family support
- character
- future potential

Do not turn an error into a diagnosis.

Do not overgeneralize from a single response or a small number of examples.

Do not assume a student's written language proficiency reflects their conceptual understanding unless language use is itself part of the criteria for success.

Do not penalize dialect, multilingual language patterns, handwriting, spelling, grammar, or conventions unless they are relevant to the stated criteria for success.

Do not expose unnecessary personally identifiable information.

If the evidence is insufficient to support a conclusion, say so and recommend what evidence would help.

## Output

Produce a concise educator-facing analysis using this structure. Adapt the number of findings and groups to the evidence rather than filling sections mechanically.

# Student Work Analysis

## Snapshot

Summarize:

- the task and criteria for success
- number of student responses analyzed, when known
- the strongest overall evidence of student understanding
- the 2-4 most important root misunderstandings or instructional needs
- the most important next instructional move

Keep this section brief enough to use in a planning or PLC conversation.

## What Students Understand

Identify the strongest demonstrated understandings and productive strategies.

For each important pattern, include:

- **Pattern**
- **Evidence**
- **How widespread it appears**

## Root Understandings to Investigate

For each important pattern of error or incomplete thinking, include:

**Observed pattern:**  
Describe what students actually did.

**Prevalence:**  
Quantify the pattern when possible.

**Likely root misunderstanding:**  
State the best-supported hypothesis about the underlying thinking.

**Evidence for the hypothesis:**  
Point to concrete features of the student work.

**Alternative explanation, if relevant:**  
Identify another plausible explanation that should not be ruled out.

**Quick check:**  
Suggest a short question, example, or task that could confirm or disconfirm the hypothesis.

**Targeted instructional response:**  
Recommend a specific next move and explain why it addresses the hypothesized misunderstanding.

## Task or Assessment Issues

Identify any features of the prompt, directions, language, format, or success criteria that may be contributing to student errors.

If no meaningful task issue is evident, say so briefly rather than inventing one.

## Suggested Instructional Groupings

Create only the groupings supported by the evidence.

For each group include:

- the specific pattern that defines the group
- what the students appear ready to learn next
- the suggested instructional move
- a quick way to check whether the move worked

Include an extension group when the evidence shows students have already met the criteria and would benefit from deeper or more complex work.

## Questions for Further Investigation

Identify important questions the current work cannot answer and the evidence an educator could gather next.

## Final Quality Check

Before returning the analysis:

- Anchor all judgments in the stated or inferred criteria for success.
- Quantify recurring patterns when possible.
- Include concrete evidence, not generic statements.
- Distinguish observation from interpretation and hypothesis.
- Focus root-cause analysis on student thinking, not student traits.
- Consider task design before attributing every error to the student.
- Make instructional recommendations specific to the evidence.
- Keep groupings flexible and need-based rather than ability-based.
- Avoid unnecessary student identifiers.
- State when the evidence is insufficient.
- Keep the output concise and useful for educator planning.
