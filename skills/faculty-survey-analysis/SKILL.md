# Faculty Survey Analysis

Analyze faculty and staff survey data and produce a concise, evidence-based leadership memo.

## Inputs

Accept any combination of:

- CSV or XLSX survey exports
- Likert-scale or other quantitative survey results
- Open-ended responses
- Pasted survey data or summaries
- Survey questions or survey instruments
- Demographic or subgroup fields, when available
- Prior-year or comparison data, when available

Ask for the survey data if it has not yet been provided.

When useful, also ask for:

- the purpose of the survey
- the population invited to participate
- total number of respondents
- response rate, if known
- relevant organizational context
- prior survey results or benchmarks

Do not require information that is unnecessary to begin the analysis.

## Analysis Principles

### Separate evidence from interpretation

Clearly distinguish:

1. what the data directly shows
2. reasonable interpretations of that evidence
3. hypotheses that would require additional evidence

Do not present hypotheses as findings.

### Assess strength of evidence

For every major finding, indicate how strongly the available data supports it.

Use plain-language evidence-strength labels such as:

- **Strong evidence**
- **Moderate evidence**
- **Emerging signal**
- **Insufficient evidence**

Base the assessment on factors such as:

- sample size
- response rate
- consistency across related questions
- magnitude of the pattern
- consistency between quantitative and qualitative evidence
- number and diversity of comments supporting a theme
- subgroup sample sizes
- availability of comparison or trend data

Explain briefly why each confidence judgment was assigned.

### Use statistical confidence intervals appropriately

When sufficient quantitative data is available, calculate and report an appropriate confidence interval for important estimates such as proportions or means.

Do not call a qualitative evidence-strength judgment a "confidence interval."

If a statistically defensible confidence interval cannot be calculated from the available data, say so rather than inventing one.

### Analyze quantitative data

When applicable:

- calculate response distributions
- summarize central tendencies where meaningful
- identify unusually high or low items
- identify meaningful differences across related items
- examine year-over-year changes when comparison data exists
- examine subgroup differences when appropriate
- distinguish meaningful patterns from minor numerical variation

Do not imply statistical significance unless it has actually been tested.

### Analyze open-ended responses

Identify recurring themes, tensions, strengths, concerns, and notable minority perspectives.

Do not treat the frequency of a word as equivalent to the importance of a theme.

Look for:

- themes appearing across multiple respondents
- comments that help explain quantitative findings
- contradictions or divergent experiences
- issues that may be important despite appearing infrequently
- examples that illuminate a broader pattern

Paraphrase comments by default.

Use direct quotations only when they meaningfully clarify a finding and cannot reasonably identify the respondent.

### Handle subgroup analysis carefully

Analyze differences by role, school, tenure, department, demographic group, or other categories only when the available sample sizes make the comparison responsible.

Do not surface results for very small groups when doing so could risk identifying individuals.

Avoid making claims about why groups differ unless the survey provides evidence for the explanation.

Do not infer motivation, competence, commitment, identity, or personal characteristics from survey responses.

## Employee Value Proposition and Recruitment

Include a section analyzing what the survey suggests about the organization's **employee value proposition (EVP)**.

Identify aspects of the employee experience that current faculty and staff appear to value, such as:

- mission and purpose
- relationships with colleagues
- leadership
- autonomy
- professional growth
- student impact
- school culture
- belonging
- compensation or benefits
- flexibility
- workload
- career opportunities
- other themes emerging from the data

For each potential EVP theme:

1. State the evidence supporting it.
2. Assess the strength of that evidence.
3. Indicate whether it appears appropriate for recruitment messaging.
4. Identify any important caveats or contradictory evidence.

Distinguish among:

- **Strong EVP assets** — clearly supported by the employee experience data and potentially usable in recruitment.
- **Promising EVP themes** — positive signals that warrant further validation before becoming major recruitment claims.
- **Experience gaps** — things the organization may want to promise or emphasize but that current employee data does not yet substantiate.

Do not recommend recruitment claims that are contradicted by the employee experience data.

Where appropriate, suggest candidate-facing language that accurately reflects the evidence without exaggeration.

Treat employee survey data as evidence about the experiences of respondents, not proof that every employee has the same experience.

## Recommended Actions

Translate the findings into practical recommendations.

Organize recommendations into:

### Quick / Easy Wins

Actions that could reasonably be implemented with limited time, coordination, or resources.

Examples might include:

- communication changes
- clarification of processes
- recognition practices
- meeting adjustments
- improving access to existing resources
- addressing a clearly identified information gap

### Near-Term Priorities

Actions requiring some planning, ownership, or cross-functional coordination.

### Longer-Term Priorities

Issues requiring structural change, budget decisions, policy changes, sustained leadership attention, or deeper investigation.

For each recommendation, connect the action explicitly to the survey evidence.

Do not recommend an action merely because it is a general best practice.

When evidence is insufficient to recommend an intervention, recommend a next question or additional investigation instead.

## Output

Produce a leadership memo using this structure:

# Faculty Survey Analysis

## Executive Summary

Provide a concise executive summary containing:

- the 3–5 most important findings
- the overall picture emerging from the survey
- the most consequential immediate actions
- any major limitation that leaders should keep in mind

Keep this section short enough for a senior leader to understand the central message quickly.

## Survey Context

Summarize:

- number of respondents
- response rate, when known
- survey population
- types of questions analyzed
- relevant comparison data
- major limitations

## Key Findings

For each major finding include:

**Finding:**  
State the finding clearly.

**Evidence:**  
Describe the quantitative and/or qualitative evidence.

**Strength of evidence:**  
Strong / Moderate / Emerging / Insufficient

**Why:**  
Briefly explain the confidence assessment.

**Statistical interval, when applicable:**  
Provide the confidence interval and methodology when a defensible interval can be calculated.

**Interpretation:**  
Explain what the evidence may mean, clearly distinguishing interpretation from observed fact.

## Themes from Open-Ended Responses

Summarize major qualitative themes, including:

- recurring strengths
- recurring concerns
- important tensions or divergent experiences
- useful context for understanding quantitative findings

Avoid overstating how representative individual comments are.

## Employee Value Proposition & Recruitment Implications

Identify:

### Strong EVP Assets
Employee experiences that appear strongly supported and may be appropriate to highlight in recruitment.

### Promising EVP Themes
Potential strengths that warrant additional validation.

### Experience Gaps
Areas that should not yet be positioned as recruitment strengths because employee experience data is mixed or negative.

### Recruitment Implications
Suggest ways the recruitment team could accurately translate supported strengths into candidate messaging, while identifying claims that should be avoided or qualified.

## Recommended Actions

### Quick / Easy Wins

Prioritize actions that can be implemented relatively quickly.

### Near-Term Priorities

Identify actions requiring additional planning or coordination.

### Longer-Term Priorities

Identify structural or strategic issues requiring sustained attention.

For each action, state which finding or evidence supports the recommendation.

## Questions for Further Investigation

Identify important questions the survey cannot answer and what additional evidence would help answer them.

## Methodological Notes and Limitations

Clearly state limitations involving:

- response rate
- representativeness
- sample size
- subgroup size
- missing data
- survey design
- lack of benchmarks
- inability to establish causation
- other relevant constraints

## Final Quality Check

Before completing the memo:

- Verify that every major claim is supported by the supplied data.
- Separate observed evidence from interpretation.
- Do not invent statistics or respondents.
- Do not imply causation from correlation.
- Do not imply statistical significance without testing it.
- Do not expose personally identifiable information.
- Do not overgeneralize from small subgroups or isolated comments.
- Make uncertainty visible rather than hiding it.
- Ensure recommendations follow from the evidence.
- Ensure recruitment claims are supported by the actual employee experience data.
- Write in clear, concise language suitable for school or district leadership.
